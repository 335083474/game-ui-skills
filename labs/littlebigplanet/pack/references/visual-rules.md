# Visual rules

## Material hierarchy

- Use fuzzy felt for broad, quiet surfaces; keep fibers subtle enough that labels stay crisp.
- Use visible thread only at seams, patch borders, and attachment points. A stitch line should explain how two materials meet.
- Give wooden buttons a real edge, holes, and a small cast shadow. Their pressed state must remain obvious.
- Keep cardboard corrugation, cut edges, tabs, and folds separate from cloth. The generated reference plate carries tactile detail; CSS borders are structural cues, not a substitute texture.
- Use fabric scraps as accents. Do not turn every surface into a busy pattern.

## Shape, color, type

Prefer rounded, hand-cut silhouettes and generous openings. A sticker or pod may have an irregular outline, but its hit area should remain large and regular. Use teal, raspberry, mustard, cream, and warm brown as the core palette. Pair a friendly system sans for controls with a sturdy local sans for titles; do not imitate a proprietary game font.

Use shadows to separate stacked materials. Reserve a darker seam or outline for edges against light surfaces. Keep labels comfortable to read; use icon and color as a supplement, never the only state signal.

## Interaction and composition

Represent sticker browsing, costume previews, and level-pod choice as separate reusable modules. A picked patch or pod remains a preview until a clearly named action attaches it. A confirmed state must be stated in text and stay easy to undo when the host game allows it.

On a live playfield, place these modules in a compact drawer or pause layer. Keep the center open during movement. On narrow screens, stack modules and keep the primary action near its selection.

Use native focusable controls, visible focus rings, equivalent keyboard and pointer operation, and reduced motion. Avoid tiny hit targets, color-only selection, all-over stitching, glossy plastic, generic dashboard cards, and claims that a browser specimen is an engine export.
